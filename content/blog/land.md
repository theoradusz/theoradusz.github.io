Below are some learnings related to buying land in the countryside of Japan, especially for foreigners or people like me who never bought real estate in their life. An exciting project of mine is to buy a piece of forest land in the middle of (almost) nowhere and build myself a retreat to enjoy life freed of worries of salaryman daily life. I am now very close of starting this project, so let me share a few things I would recommend for anyone with similar ambitions!

Maybe some of these advice will sound obvious but let it be set in (electronic) stone.

### First of all, drive and look around

The first thing you need to do is decide precisely where you want to buy land. You may have specific regions or cities in mind. If not, just find some random places on Google Maps. Anyway, you will want to go and visit a few places of interest, spend some weekends there and just drive around. Then, you can drive around and decide if the vibe of the place sounds good to you. I recommend ペンション（pensions, small and cheap japanese inns usually operated by an elder couple) for cheap and authentic stays in the countryside!

In my case, I had the following criteria: you can get there in 2 hours or less from Tokyo, there is a lot of nature, trees, mountains and rivers. Not too many people.
I had mostly three candidates: Izu peninsula, Nasu area and north Yamanashi/south Nagano Yatsugatake area. I went to all three but was quickly drawn and decided on Yamanashi after spending many weekends there!

*The sunday afternoon test*

A quick test to decide if a place is worth investing your time in: if after spending the weekend there, when the time comes to return home on sunday afternoon and you feel a strong urge or desire to stay longer, and this happens several weekends, then you can have some confidence that you will want to live in this area.

### Check local realtors

When looking for land, it may be more convenient to browse on big aggregator such as *Suumo*, but I don't recommend it: the number of postings for plots in "nature" is rather small, and many listings are outdated. My best advice would be to check local realtors, as close as possible to your target area! Some have websites where you can find information such as the size of the plot, type of land classification, price. Some even disclose the exact location of the land.

I also recommend you go in person to local realtor even if they don't have a website, even if that is more intimidating. They will be more than welcome to show you some plots after hearing your needs.

### Go inspect the land yourself

Obviously for land plots, unlike appartments or houses, you don't need a key: virtually anyone can trespass into the open land. This means that after inquiring by email about a plot of land, the flow was usually the following:
1. go to this address and inspect the plot of land yourself at your convenience
2. if you have questions afterwards, send them and the realtor answers
3. if you are still interested at this stage, you can do another visit on-site with the realtor that will provide further details and explanations
4. if you are decided on the land, you can apply and start the acquisition process

Personally, having been able to spend time on the land by myself played a huge role to inform my choice. As a matter of fact, the first time I went to see this land, I didn't like it. It was cold, almost night time, we did not have much time and I had caught a cold. I was in a really bad condition and didn't really enjoy the visit. But after a good night of sleep, I decided to give it another chance. I returned the next morning and instantly fell in love with it.

That's why I highly recommend you go several times to your plots of interests before you make a move!

### Learning japanese key words and concepts

There are a few specifies to japanese real estate. Here is a short lexicon of word that will be useful to understand when starting your journey.

- 都市計画区域 (toshi keikaku kuiki): city planning zone. it is important to get a feel for zoning laws here, especially "city planning zones". Something that was not intuitive for me: if you are outside of the city planning zone, you are basically free to build. If you are inside, then there are several possibilities: either you are in a zone where they actively don't want you to build (hazard zone, low density zone) and this may be a problem. Or you are in a "densification" zone where you will be able to build, but with more constraints on the type of building. Your realtor will be the key person to explain about your land's case.

- 山林 (sanrin): moutain and woods. if you buy a forest land plot like me, there are high chances it will be classified as a montain and woods area. This means: you will probably won't pay tax on it as long as it remains like this, but you won't be able to build on it as it is. You will have to go through the administrative process of converting it or part of it to a residential plot, which is totally doable.

- 坪 (tsubo): the main size unit used to measure plots of land. One tsubo is equal to two tatami mats, or 3.3 square meters.

- ハザードマップ (hazado mappu): hazard map. a must check for any real estate acquisition in Japan: are we located in a natural disaster hazard zone or not? Your realtor will tell you about it, but I recommend understanding major concepts such as "yellow zones" or "red zones" before any acquisition.

- 公図 (kōzu): this is the cadastral map showing the boundaries of your plot and neighboring parcels. It is important to understand how many other plots are bordering yours and try to get a sense if they could be developed later or not. Also useful to compare with hazard maps to check if any part of your land falls into a risk zone.

- 測量 (sokuryō): land surveying. If you buy a large plot and build a house on it, you might end up paying significant taxes on the entire area. A common strategy is to divide the land into a residential portion and a 山林 portion, so that you only pay residential taxes on the part where your house stands. But to do this, you need a proper sokuryō before splitting the land, and this costs time and money. It can be part of the negotiation to have the seller conduct the survey before the sale.

- 伐採 (bassai): tree clearing. This can cost a lot if you have many large trees, so anticipate this in your budget. Some people recommend clearing everything for "easy access" which could cost 500万円 or more, but that totally defeats the point of buying a forest plot! You probably just want to remove the necessary space for building a house and a small garden. I recommend getting quotes (相見積もり) from different companies to compare prices. Accessibility and tree size can really change the cost. Also, if you agree to let them take the wood, it can be cheaper.

### Bring builders to your (future) plot
They will tell you if it is possible to build, give you their opinion for free. You can usually go pretty far in the process, get a first design/madori and rough quote for free, before signing any contract.

### *Call* the realtors
Much quicker and efficient than email

### Study the kōzu map
Understand where your parcel is located, who are your neighbors and how your land relates to theirs. Are they likely to develop their plot in the future? Is there a road or river nearby that could affect access? This document is key to understanding the bigger picture around your little piece of forest.

### Try to estimate costs and potential issues
Beyond the price of the land itself, there are many additional costs to anticipate. Water network connection (or well digging), septic tank installation, electricity connection, ground stability improvements if you're on a slope, tree clearing as mentioned above, and of course taxes. Try to get rough estimates for all of these before committing, so you don't end up with unpleasant surprises.

### Engage with local people
Talk to locals and business owners around your target area. You will get a feel for the community and whether it's the kind of place you want to be part of. In our case, we were warmly welcomed by people running a local cafe nearby, which showed us the area was quite dynamic and friendly. This kind of interaction can tell you more about a place than any website or realtor ever could.

### Enjoy the process
Finally, don't rush it. It's actually fun to visit many plots, and it allows you to explore and discover the area in depth. There is no pressure to make a decision quickly. Take your time, go back multiple times, enjoy the journey of finding your perfect spot.

Thank you for reading, I hope some of this was useful! If you have any questions feel free to reach out.
