I was recently interviewed by a colleague. He wanted to hear about my  experience as a researcher in the field of disaster mitigation in Japan, coming here with a french point of view. I am seizing this occasion today to write about my background and personal insights on this matter.

There is a certain disconnect between what I had imagined and hoped I would be doing as a researcher, compared to what actually happened.

Back in 2020, I was a student in urban engineering in France. My main interest was in how to design a city that would be pleasant to live, as well as resilient to climate change and disasters. I was already seing myself playing *SimCity* or *Cities Skyline* in real life, steering multi million dollar construction projects from above with holy urban engineering principles. The reality was different. Public-private project financing, public consultations, roadmaps from the city, the region, the metropolis, the train company, the office developer. A
millefeuille of institutions, government agencies and large corporations with conflicting interests. The result most often don't quite align with making the city nice and resilient for all the inhabitants.

In engineering school classes, we are commonly explained that disasters and disruptions of high impact will increase in frequency over time with climate change. This is the case even in France where usually nothing much happens. This is where I felt a disconnect with urban engineering I had been taught. The cities, even newer ones, are not designed to sustain large scale disasters. I did not notice a major concern of disaster countermeasures, as there is in Japan.

In a convenient concordance with my main hobby of the time, Japanese language learning, I decided to go study abroad in Japan, to learn about urban engineering and disaster mitigation (*bousai*).
Disaster mitigation has many different aspects. We can roughly divide in two main categories: hard and soft disaster countermeasures.
Hard countermeasures consists in making infrastructure and buildings physically stronger to natural disasters: retrofitting, stronger concrete, huge walls on the coast against tsunami, extra redundancy in infrastructures.
Soft countermeasures, on the other hand, are the ones that don't rely on built infrastructure but more on the organization of the people: alert systems, land use, education, insurance and economy related measures, for example.

I was mostly interested in the softer side, especially what we call "community oriented city-building" (*machizukuri*). I wanted to understand its true meaning and how it took place in Japan. I imagined myself an adventurer (field researcher) exploring desolated villages of post-2011 disaster northern Japan. I would be interviewing the locals (with my weak language skills of the time) and deriving a complete typology of disaster resilient urban patterns and community efforts. This field is far from being this simple, so this was a complete illusion. But I did not know it at the time.

Contrary to what I had imagined, I ended up in a disaster mitigation centered *civil engineering* lab. Everyone there was mostly focused on concrete building simulation and earthquake retrofitting. I realized that I wouldn't be able to do what I had originally planned. Fair enough, I went with the flow and switch to spatial data analysis using open data. I ended up enjoying it quite a lot, there was a lot to learn in understanding the spatiality of risk and vulnerability, and open datasets were still under-exploited for these purposes. Still, there is some disappointment I couldn't obtain my deep understanding of community building in Japan.

In spite of that, I managed to survive 5 whole years in Japan. Being an bystander of daily life here, and being myself interested to move to the countryside in the near future, I have gained some understanding about Japanese communities there.

As far I understood, *machizukuri* community building consists in a subset, or all of what I am listing below.
- local summer festivals
- neighbour association (*chonaikai*)
- shopping street (*shotengai*) associations
- local businesses initiatives
- local government initiatives
- local JA (agricultural cooperative) rice collectivization systems
- *akiya* renovation groups
- some NPOs and researcher's initiatives
So, a patchwork of efforts without a real coordinating driving force. Its primary goal is not to focus on city resilience, it has a lot of fragmented interests (see the similarity with my impression on urban engineering in France). However as I said above, these are just the remarks of a simple observer!

Now, let me conclude with my two cents on the state of the research on disaster mitigation.

First of all, the important data we need to accurately analyze the risks is not available to researchers. I am thinking about strategic infrastructure data, such as electricity and water networks. This is a big issue that will hinder improving the robustness of current infrastructure.

Another point I want to make is that disaster mitigation should not only be about earthquake and tsunami countermeasures. While a blockade in the Strait of Ormuz doesn't have the same immediate impact, it is likely that the consequences will affect Japan's economy for years. If a foreign country situation has the risk to cut our energy supply we are 95% dependent on, the consequences can be the ones of the magnitude of a disaster. Furthermore, if a major natural disaster hits after we have depleted our strategic energy reserves to face the blockade, the country will be extremely vulnerable.

That is why I believe we should strive to innovate to create more resilience and independence on the most strategic infrastructure, not just to create increasingly complex and non-vital technology.
